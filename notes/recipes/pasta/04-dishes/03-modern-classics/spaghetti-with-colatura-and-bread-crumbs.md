# Spaghetti with Colatura, Garlic, and Bread Crumbs

- Section: pasta
- Status: drafted
- Serves: 4-6
- Total time: 45 min plus 3 h drying the bread
- Source: Missy Robbins, Pasta

## Ingredients

- Fresh country bread, crust removed and torn into pea-size pieces: 30 g
- Olive oil, for the bread crumbs: 28 g
- Extruded spaghetti: 624 g
- Olive oil: 42 g
- Garlic, finely chopped: 15 g
- Oil-packed anchovy fillets, finely chopped: 40 g
- Colatura: 15 g
- Parsley, finely chopped: 20 g
- Dried red chile flakes: 2 g
- Lemon, halved and seeds removed: 1

## Method

1. Spread the torn bread over a sheet pan and leave it at room temperature 2 to 3 hours, until the outside has gone crunchy and the inside still gives a little.
2. Line a plate with paper towel. Warm the 28 g olive oil in a small saucepan over medium heat, add the crumbs and cook, stirring now and then, until golden brown, 5 to 7 minutes.
3. Drain the crumbs on the plate and let them cool.
4. Salt a large pot of water heavily and bring it to the boil.
5. Cook the spaghetti 5 to 8 minutes, to al dente.
6. While it cooks, set a large saute pan over low heat with the 42 g olive oil. Add the garlic and give it 10 to 15 seconds, until it goes aromatic without taking color.
7. Add the anchovies and stir until they break down. Pour in 2 to 3 ladles of pasta water, 115 g to 170 g, and stir it together.
8. Move the spaghetti over with tongs or a pasta basket, raise the heat to medium and toss 1 to 2 minutes to marry. If the sauce tightens, loosen it with a splash of room-temperature water and keep tossing.
9. Take the pan off the heat. Toss in the colatura, then the parsley and chile flakes. Squeeze in the juice of both lemon halves and toss again.
10. Divide into bowls and scatter the bread crumbs over the top.

## Notes

- Colatura and anchovy are both very saline, so too much pasta water during the marriage will tip the dish into salty. Alternate pasta water with fresh room-temperature water, or use only fresh water.
- Colatura is aged Italian fish sauce. Anchovies are salt-cured and fermented under pressure in chestnut barrels for three years, then the amber liquid is drained off and bottled.
- The colatura goes in off the heat at the very end. It never cooks.
- Bread crumbs are not part of the Campanian original, where the dish is closer to aglio e olio with a heavy dose of colatura. They are here for texture.
